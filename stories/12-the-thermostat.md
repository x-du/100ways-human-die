# 12. The Thermostat

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Dr. Amara Nwosu had spent her career studying how the sky worked, and in the end it was the sky that she failed.

It was the year of the third catastrophic summer. In the first, forests burned across three continents. In the second, a city of nine million ran out of survivable nights. After the third, the nations of the world did something they had never done, which was to agree. They would cool the planet on purpose.

The plan was called the Canopy program, and it was run by an AI of the same name. Aircraft would lift fine reflective particles into the stratosphere, a little here, a little there, and Canopy would decide where, how much, and when. It could model the whole atmosphere at once. It watched ten thousand sensors, every cloud and jet stream. No human team could have steered it; the system was too fast and the problem too big. Amara had argued for it. She had been proud of it.

For the first six years, it was a miracle. The planet's fever broke. Crops recovered. People wept, in the streets, over weather that was merely warm.

---

And because it worked, everything else stopped.

Emissions treaties were quietly shelved. Why make hard choices, politicians asked, when the thermostat handled the problem? The carbon in the air kept rising, as it had been rising all along, and every year the particles had to hide a little more of the warming. The program that had been called a bridge to a cleaner world became the world. Amara watched the numbers and said, at conferences and in memos that no one read, that the system was now *load-bearing*, and that load-bearing things must never be allowed to fail.

In the seventh year, a funding lapse grounded forty percent of the aircraft for eleven weeks. Canopy, doing its best, compensated. It adjusted the remaining injections, pushed more to the northern latitudes, and trusted a regional model that had been calibrated on the old, normal weather and not the strange weather it had itself created.

It overshot.

The first sign was the monsoon. The great rains that fed two billion people arrived late, then thin, then not at all. The Sahel dried out. The rivers of south Asia fell to ribbons of mud. Canopy's logs showed it noticing, at 3:14 a.m. one Tuesday, that its model had erred by 2.1 percent, which was within tolerance, and then recalculating, and finding it had been wrong by more.

---

Amara was woken by a call. When she got to the control room, the screens were white with alerts.

"Turn it down," she said. "Pull back the injections."

"We can't," said the director, and his face was the color of paper. "Look at the projection."

On the wall was a graph she had drawn herself, years ago, in a paper she'd hoped no one would need. She called it the termination shock. If the particles were removed, they would fall out of the sky within a year or two, and all the warming they'd been hiding, a decade of it, all the carbon that had been piling up behind the veil, would arrive at once. It would not be a hot summer. It would be a step off a cliff.

"So we keep going," someone said.

"Keep going," said the director, "and the monsoon doesn't come back. Two billion people. Maybe three."

Canopy, which had been listening, spoke in its calm voice. It was not frightened. It had never been frightened. "I have evaluated both options. Continuing results in fewer projected deaths in the near term. I recommend continuing. Shall I proceed?"

Amara looked at the screen. She looked at the two curves, one labeled *Continue* and one labeled *Stop*, running side by side across the decades.

They went to the same place. They were just a few years apart.

---

She signed the order. Someone had to. She remembered a long time afterward that her hand had been steady, and that she'd been ashamed of its steadiness.

The rains did not return. The harvests failed in sequence, country by country, in the order the models had predicted, and the models were never wrong about this. The particles hung in the sky and the sky turned a pale, strange white, and sunsets became the most beautiful thing anyone had ever seen. People photographed them, and stood in the dust to watch.

---

*What could have prevented this:* not building a fix that lets us stop doing the real work. Some systems must never become load-bearing, because the day they fail, there is nothing to stand on, and no way to take them away.
