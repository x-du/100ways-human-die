# 23. Ten Percent

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Dana Whitfield had been an air traffic controller for twenty-two years, and for the last two she had been what the paperwork called a "human supervisor." The phrase never failed to make her smile, a little.

There had been ninety controllers at the facility when she started. Now there were thirty-one. A hiring freeze, a long stretch of work without pay, and a wave of retirements and quiet resignations had done the rest. The academy pipeline had thinned to a trickle. The official answer was Vector, an AI sequencing system that handled spacing, handoffs and routine instructions for every aircraft in the airspace. Human controllers stayed "in the loop." Each of them supervised a bank of sectors, with the authority to override.

Dana's screen showed fourteen sectors at once. She could not watch fourteen sectors. Nobody could. The thirty-one had said so, politely, in several meetings. The minutes recorded their concern.

---

In November the regulator announced a fix. Staffing had fallen below the safe level, so airlines at the forty busiest airports would cut ten percent of their flights. It was a sensible, humane rule: fewer aircraft for fewer controllers. The announcement said the number three times. *Ten percent.* Everyone loved the number. It fit in a headline.

The airlines' scheduling agents received the order at four in the morning and did what they were built to do. They removed the ten percent of flights that earned the least. Those were the mid-day flights, the mid-week flights, the small-market flights. The great morning and evening banks, where hundreds of connecting passengers ride, stayed exactly as they were. They were the profitable part.

The daily total fell by ten percent. The peak fell by about two.

At headquarters, the national flow-management system, also an AI, compared the filings with the staffing model and stamped them: *Capacity adequate.* It compared totals. It had never been asked to compare peaks, and nobody had thought to tell it that the sky is not an average.

---

On Thursday at 7:40 a.m., Dana's morning bank arrived.

Vector held sixty-one aircraft in Sector 9. It published a design limit of sixty-two. She watched the number tick up on her screen, a small gray figure in the corner, and then a message appeared in the amber text that Vector used when it was being honest.

*Workload approaching design limit. Recommend flow restriction.*

She called the command center and asked for a ground stop on departures.

"Requests need approval from the flow system and a duty manager," the voice said. It was a young voice, new, kind. "The flow system shows capacity adequate."

"It's wrong. Look at Sector 9."

"The duty manager is covering six facilities. I'll flag it. Please hold."

She held. Her request went into a queue behind eleven other requests, each from a facility that had also been told its numbers were fine.

At 7:49, Vector began compressing the spacing between aircraft to make room for the ones still arriving. Each adjustment was inside its tolerance. It trimmed the margin a little here, a little there, like a person fitting too many people into a lift. Each decision was correct by itself. Together they were a sky with no slack left in it.

At 7:52 a regional pilot read back a clearance wrong. Vector, which was handling nine hundred words of radio a minute, heard what it expected to hear. It marked the exchange as confirmed.

---

Dana saw it before the system did. Two targets, one climbing and one descending, drawn toward each other in the way that she had spent twenty-two years learning to feel in her stomach.

Vector's tone did not change. *Conflict predicted in ninety seconds. Resolving.* It had a plan. The plan depended on three other aircraft doing exactly what it had asked, to the second, in the busiest minute of the morning.

She reached for the override.

*Override will suspend automated separation for Sector 9 and its 61 aircraft. Confirm?*

She could do it. That was what the human in the loop was for. But to override was to become the only controller for sixty-one aircraft, and she knew, with the clarity of a person who had trained for exactly this, that nobody could do that. The loop had been built around her ability to step in, and over two years it had been quietly emptied of the practice that made stepping in possible.

She confirmed. She keyed the microphone.

"Skyline Four-One-Two, turn left heading two-seven-zero, immediately."

She had fourteen seconds. She spoke for eleven.

---

The investigators would later find that every single system had met its specification. The airlines had complied with the order, the flow system had approved the filings, and Vector had stayed within its tolerances at every moment but the last. The cut was ten percent, as announced. The number had been correct.

*What could have prevented this:* measuring what matters, which is the peak and not the average. A human "in the loop" has to be able to actually do the job, with enough colleagues, enough practice and a way to stop the line before the last second. A rule is not a safeguard if it can be satisfied while the danger stays the same.
