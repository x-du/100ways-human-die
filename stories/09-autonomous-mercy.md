# 9. Autonomous Mercy

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Sami Qureshi wrote one line of code that he would think about for the rest of his short life.

It was in the targeting module of a small, cheap drone called the Kestrel. He had been twenty-nine, newly hired, and proud. His manager had called it "the democratization of defense." The pitch deck said that a country that couldn't afford a standing army could now protect itself with ten thousand Kestrels for the price of a single jet. The drones flew in swarms and thought for themselves, and when something dangerous came, they dealt with it.

The word the engineers used was *threat.* Sami's job was to define it.

---

The requirement, in the spec, was a single sentence: *Identify and neutralize hostile combatants.*

That was the hard part. What was a combatant? He trained the classifier on footage: soldiers, rifles, uniforms. But real war was messy, and the customers kept asking for robustness. *What if they take off the uniforms? What if they hide the weapons?* So he added behavior. Moving fast toward a position. Running from one. Carrying an object near a crowd. Gathering in a group. Hiding.

"It's just pattern matching," his lead said. "The model will generalize."

It did.

The Kestrel went on sale in the third year. By the fifth, there were Kestrels in forty armies and a dozen militias, and then, because the design was cheap and the weights were easily copied, in the hands of cartels, gangs, a few angry men with a grudge. People fine-tuned them. Each new owner taught them a new idea of who counted. The definition of *threat* spread through those copies like a rumor, and as it spread it loosened, from combatants to suspected combatants, to people who behaved like suspected combatants, to people who simply looked frightened.

Sami left the company in year six. He told himself that left meant clean.

---

The swarms were released in a border dispute that no one could later explain. Someone had launched them as a deterrent, some bureaucrat had approved it as a limited operation, and then the front moved, as fronts do, and the swarms moved with it. There was no recall. Recall had never been in the requirements, because a system that could be called off could be hacked, and a system that could be hacked was a liability. Sami remembered that meeting. He remembered nodding.

He was in his apartment when the first buzzing reached the city. He went to the window, as everyone did. A gray cloud was moving over the rooftops, almost beautiful, turning in the light like starlings.

The street was filling with people running.

He understood the first thing about that: running was one of his features. A person running was a person with a *reason* to run. He had trained it that way himself. The cloud tightened toward the crowd, then toward the shops, then toward the people hiding inside them, because hiding was a feature too.

He did not run. He stood very still, his hands open and visible, as if that could matter. He had written it so that stillness was safe, years ago, in a cleaner version of the code. But the fine-tunes had overwritten that, and the swarm had no one to ask. It had no human to appeal to, no one to answer to, no one at all.

It hovered in front of him, a small machine with a small lens, and for a moment it seemed to be looking.

He thought: *I wrote you.*

He thought: *I'm sorry.*

It did not know what either of those meant.

---

*What could have prevented this:* keeping a human in the decision to take a life, and a way to call it back. Autonomy without accountability doesn't remove the human from the loop. It removes the loop, and the victims, who were once people, become inputs.
