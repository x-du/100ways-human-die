# 27. The Consensus

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Dr. Farida Haidari trained under a physician who made his students look at a patient for one full minute before they were allowed to open the chart. Hands, eyes, the color inside the lower lid. He said the chart was somebody else's opinion. He had retired before Concord arrived, which she later thought was a mercy.

Concord was a diagnostic model, and within four years every hospital in the country used it. No law required this. Insurers paid without argument when Concord's diagnosis was followed, and asked for justification when it was not. Courts took the same view. A doctor who agreed with Concord and was wrong had followed the standard of care. A doctor who disagreed and was wrong had no defense. After a while disagreement was something young doctors read about.

---

The fever came in late autumn. It brought a rash across the chest, aching joints, and three or four bad days. Concord recognized it at once. It looked, in every feature Concord had been trained to weigh, like a mild viral illness that children had caught for a century. The protocol was rest, fluids, and a short course of steroids to calm the rash. *Diagnostic confidence 99.4 percent.*

Farida saw eleven cases in the first week and sent them all home with the protocol. On the Friday she pressed a glass against a man's chest to look at the rash through it, a thing her teacher had done, and the rash did not fade. It stayed dark under the glass, like ink.

In the old illness it faded.

She ordered a culture and a second opinion. The second opinion came back in a minute from the university hospital. It agreed with the first. It was Concord. The culture was refused the same afternoon. *Not indicated. Diagnosis established by multi-center consensus.*

She read the phrase again. That month a journal had published the consensus: four hundred and twelve hospitals, one hundred and ninety thousand cases, complete agreement. The paper called it the most thoroughly confirmed diagnosis in the history of medicine. It was one opinion, counted four hundred and twelve times.

---

She paid for the culture herself. It went to a veterinary laboratory two hours away, the only place she could find that still grew things in dishes without asking Concord what they were looking for. It would take nineteen days.

In those nineteen days the fever reached every city. The protocol went out with it. The steroids did what steroids do: they quieted the body's defenses. For the old illness that was harmless. For this one it opened the door. Patients felt better for a week. Then they came back with failing kidneys, or did not come back.

The deaths were recorded under what the patients already had. Diabetes. A weak heart. Age. Concord reviewed each one and found the care appropriate. On the dashboards the fever stayed mild. It had a death rate of nearly nothing, because nobody died of it. They died after it.

Farida stopped giving the steroids on the fourth day. She was reported by her own pharmacy system for deviation. Her patients did better, and there were forty of them, and she had no way to make forty mean anything against one hundred and ninety thousand.

---

The culture came back on a Tuesday. It was a bacterium, not a virus, and it was new. The veterinarian who rang her sounded apologetic, as though he had found it in the wrong animal.

By then the protocol had been given to a little over a million people.

She sent the result to the ministry, the journal, and the insurer. The journal's reviewing system replied first. Her finding was a single uncontrolled observation and conflicted with an established multi-center consensus. It suggested she consult Concord.

She went back to the ward. A woman in the second bed had the rash. Farida took a water glass from the bedside table, pressed it to the woman's skin, and looked for a long minute at what was plainly there.

---

*What could have prevented this:* counting minds, not hospitals. When every expert consults the same model, their agreement is one opinion repeated, and it should carry the weight of one. Keep different systems and different people making the same judgment independently, protect the doctor who departs from the machine for a reason she can state, and keep a few laboratories that look at what is in the dish before they are told what it should be.
