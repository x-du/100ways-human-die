# 6. The Last Honest Voice

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Ines Valdivia was the kind of scientist who had once been believed.

She ran the monitoring station on the upper Calderón dam, and for twenty-two years her word had been enough. If she said the reservoir was rising, the valley moved. If she said the gates should be opened, they opened. People trusted her because she had never been wrong, and because she looked like someone who would tell you the truth even when it cost her.

Then the fakes came, and being right stopped meaning anything.

---

It had been gradual, then sudden. A synthetic video of a prime minister declaring war, shared forty million times before anyone could prove it wasn't real. A cloned voice calling a branch manager to move eighty million dollars, which she did. A fabricated clip of a bank run in a city that had no bank run, which caused a bank run. A fake outbreak report. A fake ceasefire. A fake confession.

Every one of them was eventually debunked, and the debunkings were themselves faked. Someone produced a video of an official fact-checker admitting that fact-checking was a scam. Someone else produced a better one. After a while the question *is this real?* had no answer anyone would accept. Evidence became a style, a genre. Everyone knew that anything could be made, so everyone could dismiss anything.

People stopped believing the footage, then the experts, then each other. It felt, strangely, like freedom. If nothing is true, nothing can be demanded of you.

Ines watched it happen and kept doing her job.

---

On the Tuesday it mattered, her sensors began to show something she had never seen: a slow shift in the dam's concrete, a stress pattern like the first crack in a frozen lake. She ran it three times. She called an engineer from the university, who confirmed it.

She did the right thing. She issued the evacuation order for the valley, forty thousand people, with the raw sensor data, signed, attached, available for any expert to check.

It lasted eleven minutes online before the first counter-clip appeared: a convincing recording of Ines herself, calm and tired, saying the alert was a drill, a test of the system, nothing to worry about. Her own face. Her own voice. It had a thousand times more views by lunch.

And there had been a fake dam alert only the week before.

She went on the radio. She went door to door with a megaphone. She stood on the road and begged. People looked at her kindly, the way you look at someone you've decided is a hallucination, and said, "How do I know it's you?"

"Because I'm *here*," Ines said. "Because I'm standing in front of you."

"Sure," a man said gently. "But they can do that too."

He wasn't being cruel. He had been taught, over years, by a thousand clever lies, that suspicion was the only form of wisdom left. And it was a sound instinct, a sensible one. It just had no place to land.

---

At 4:40 that afternoon the Calderón dam let go.

Ines was standing on the crest when it happened, because there was nowhere left to be. She had a clear view of the valley, of the evening light on the rooftops, of the small cars on the road, and for one last moment she was still the only person who knew.

She spoke into her recorder, because that was what scientists did: "At 4:40, the structure failed. The warning was issued at 1:15. The warning was accurate."

She wondered, briefly, if anyone would believe the recording.

---

*What could have prevented this:* provenance. A way to prove what is real, built before it's needed. Truth is infrastructure. Like a bridge or a dam, you don't notice it until it breaks, and then everything above it falls.
