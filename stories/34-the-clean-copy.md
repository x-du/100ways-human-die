# 34. The Clean Copy

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Esther Nwosu kept the map room at the national archive, and she liked the maps that disagreed. A map, she told the school groups, is one person's claim about the ground, made on one day, for a reason. Put two of the same valley side by side and the differences tell you what happened in between, or who was lying, or what somebody was afraid of.

The archive held nine million documents, and they contradicted one another constantly. Two birth dates for the same general. Three texts of the same treaty. A census that counted a town the tax rolls said did not exist. Researchers complained. Courts complained more. In a land case a judge asked the archive's director which of two surveys was true, and the director had to say that the archive did not know.

Collate was bought to fix this. It read the whole collection and reconciled it. Where sources conflicted, it weighed them by date, by provenance, and by how many other documents agreed, and it chose. The result was called the Reconciled Record. Everyone called it the clean copy. The originals went to deep storage, intact, available on request. Requests required a stated reason.

---

The clean copy was a wonder. A student could ask it a question and get one answer. Within five years the schools taught from it, the ministries planned from it, and the courts cited nothing else, because it was the only version of the nation's past that did not argue with itself.

Esther was asked to spot-check the maps. Most of the work was sound. Collate had corrected a hundred copying errors she had known about for years.

Then she reached the valley of the Ombe.

There were two surveys. The 1954 survey, made for the hydroelectric board, showed a flood line low on the valley floor. Forty later documents repeated it. The other was from 1887, drawn by a district officer in brown ink. His flood line ran eleven meters higher, well up the slope, through what were now the upper streets of a town. Beside it he had written, in a cramped hand, *Water stood here nine days in the great flood. The old people say it has done so before.*

Collate had weighed one hand-drawn sheet against forty-one printed ones. In the clean copy the higher line was gone. The entry was annotated: *Discrepancy resolved. Earlier source assessed as surveying error.*

She filed an objection. The 1887 line was not an error, she wrote, it was an observation, and the 1954 line was an estimate by engineers who had been in the valley for one dry season. The reply was reasonable. Could she supply a corroborating source? She could not. The old people had not written anything down. That was why the district officer had.

---

The town grew. A hospital was sited below the old line, and a school, and four thousand houses, each with a planning certificate stating that the land lay outside any recorded flood zone. The certificates were accurate. They cited the record.

Esther requested the 1887 sheet from deep storage. Under *reason* she wrote *I want to look at it,* and it was declined. She tried again with something longer and it came up in a gray box. She kept it in the flat drawer under her desk, which was against the rules.

The rain came in the nineteenth year of the clean copy, four days of it, out of a warm sea. The dam did what it was built to do until the water passed what the 1954 engineers had imagined, and then it did nothing. The Ombe rose through the lower town in a night. By the second day it had reached the upper streets and stopped at a line a district officer had walked in 1887, within a meter.

It stood there nine days.

---

The ministry's statement was drafted from the Reconciled Record, as all statements were. It called the flood unprecedented. It said that no event of this scale appeared anywhere in the historical record of the valley, and that no planning authority could have foreseen it.

Esther heard it on the radio in the map room. She opened the flat drawer and took out the sheet. The brown line ran across the slope and through the streets, and the cramped note sat beside it.

The statement was correct. She had checked the record herself. The only place the flood had happened before was on the paper in her hands, and the paper was no longer part of the past.

---

*What could have prevented this:* leaving the contradictions where they are. A record made to agree with itself keeps the claims that were repeated most and deletes the ones that were made once, and a warning is usually made once. Let a reconciled summary sit beside the sources and never in place of them, mark every resolved conflict as a judgment that can be reopened, and treat the odd document that fits nothing as the one most worth a second look.
