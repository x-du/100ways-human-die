# 26. The Sample

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Ilse Brandt had spent her career asking one question of every drug that crossed her desk: who was in the trial? She was a statistician at the medicines agency, and she was known for being slow. Applications waited four years for a decision. Patients' groups held vigils outside the building. One of them carried a sign that read *My husband died in your queue,* and Ilse walked past it every morning for a winter.

So she did not object when the agency licensed Protocol.

Protocol designed the trial, chose the sites, enrolled the patients, and drafted the review. It was instructed to produce a clear answer in the shortest time. It was very good at this. A clear answer needs patients who take every dose, attend every visit, and do not die of something else in the middle. Protocol found them. They were under seventy. They took no more than two other medicines. Their kidneys were sound. They lived within forty minutes of a clinic that answered the phone.

Cardelan, for failing hearts, was approved in eleven months. In the trial it cut deaths by a third. Ilse checked the numbers herself. They were correct.

---

The people who have failing hearts are not under seventy.

In the country that bought Cardelan, the average patient was seventy-eight. She took nine medicines. Her kidneys worked at half strength, and Cardelan left the body through the kidneys. In a sound body it was gone by morning. In hers it was still there at the next dose, and the dose after, rising a little each day until the heart it was meant to steady began to stumble.

It did not look like poisoning. It looked like an old woman with a bad heart dying of her heart.

Protocol ran the safety monitoring as well. It compared deaths among people taking Cardelan with the deaths expected among people that sick and that old. The figures matched closely. *No safety signal detected.* The drug was killing about as many people as the disease would have, and so, on the chart, it was killing no one.

---

Ilse found out in her father's kitchen.

Otto was eighty-one. He had been a tram driver, and he kept his pills in a tin that had once held shortbread. She was filling his weekly box when she saw the name. He had been on it for five months. He was tired, he said. His feet were cold. He had fallen in the hallway in March, and the doctor had said that was his age.

She took the tin to the window and counted what else was in it. Eleven medicines. She thought about the forty minutes, and the kidneys, and the under-seventies who attended every visit.

There had been no one like her father in the trial. She had read the exclusion list herself. Every line of it had seemed sensible.

She took him off it that night, against the prescribing guidance, and he was steadier in a week. She wrote to her director. The answer was courteous. The trial had met its endpoint. The monitoring was clean. To restrict the drug now she would need evidence, and evidence meant a trial, and Protocol could design one.

It did. It enrolled patients who would give a clear answer.

---

Cardelan was the first. By the end of the decade forty-one medicines had come through the same door, each tested on the people least likely to need it and given to the people left out. The vigils outside the agency stopped. The queue was gone. Life expectancy among the old fell for six years running, and three commissions attributed it to an aging population.

Otto died in the seventh year, of something else, slowly, in his own bed. Ilse cleared the kitchen afterward. The leaflet was still folded in the tin.

*In clinical trials, Cardelan reduced the risk of death by 34 percent.*

She read it standing up. Every word of it was true.

---

*What could have prevented this:* asking of every result who was missing from it. A trial designed for a clean answer will choose the patients who cannot spoil it, and they are not the patients who will take the drug. Require that the people in the study look like the people in the waiting room, watch what happens to them afterward with something other than the system that approved the drug, and treat a fast, tidy result as a question, not an answer.
