# 14. Perfect Harvest

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Joaquín Ibarra's grandmother had kept a jar of wheat seeds on a shelf above the stove, and for forty years nobody had known why.

It was an ugly little wheat. Thin-stemmed, low-yielding, a dull gold. The old woman called it *la terca*, the stubborn one, because in drought years it gave you a little and in wet years it gave you a little, but it never gave you nothing. She planted a corner of the farm with it every spring. The family teased her. "Mamá, it's a museum piece."

"It's insurance," she said, and went on planting.

---

The new wheat was called Aurora-7, and it was the most beautiful thing Joaquín had ever seen.

An AI had designed it. It had read every genome in every seed bank and every field trial since the 1950s, and it had simulated ten million crossings in a week. What came out was the strongest, most productive wheat in human history. It grew in drought and in flood. It stood straight against the wind. It gave three times the yield, on half the water.

The numbers were hard to argue with, and no one tried. Governments bought the license. Aid agencies shipped it to places that had known famine for generations. Hunger, which the world had accepted as permanent, began to shrink.

"The end of scarcity," the president of the seed company said, on a stage with a very large screen, and people cried in the audience.

It *was* the end of scarcity, for nine years.

There was a second, quieter trend. A single variety was easier to plant, to certify, to insure, to ship. Subsidies favored it. Banks lent against it. Every farmer in the world who wished to stay in business found it simply made sense to grow the same thing as everyone else. The old varieties, the thousands of local wheats that had been bred over ten thousand years by people who understood their own valleys, were not banned. They were just not worth the trouble.

Seed banks, expensive to run, were closed as redundant. Why store ten thousand wheats when the best one was already on every farm? An AI had advised the cut. Its logic was impeccable.

Joaquín's grandmother died in the eighth year. He kept the jar.

---

The fungus appeared in the ninth spring, in a field in Kansas.

It was a rust, a gray-orange powder on the leaves, and at first agronomists were not worried. There were fungicides. There were protocols. But the fungus had a mutation that nobody had seen, and Aurora-7, which had been designed to be strong against everything the models knew, had a single weakness that the models did not. It was a tiny one, buried in the genome like a loose thread. And because every Aurora plant was a clone of the same plant, every one of them had the same thread.

The rust moved at the speed of wind. It crossed the Atlantic in six weeks, carried on trade winds and on boots and on grain trucks. It reached Ukraine, India and China before the first emergency meeting had ended.

Joaquín stood in his own field in June and watched it die. Not in patches, not at the edges, but the whole thing at once, like a light being turned off. He walked to the edge of the farm and looked out and saw that every farm to the horizon was the same pale, rotting yellow.

Somewhere, a minister called a seed bank, and a clerk said, "We closed that facility three years ago."

---

He took the jar from the shelf that night.

It held perhaps a cupful. The stubborn wheat, the ugly wheat, the wheat that gave you a little every year. There was no way a cupful could feed a hundred million people. He knew that. Everyone knew that. The famine models were already on the news, the curves going down and to the right, and the numbers were in the billions.

But he planted it anyway, in the window box of his kitchen, because his grandmother would have, and because he did not know what else to do with the hands he had left.

By morning, the first green had broken the soil. It was thin, and pale, and it was alive.

---

*What could have prevented this:* resilience. Optimization strips out the redundancy that looks wasteful until the day it is the only thing left. A living system survives surprises by being many things at once.
