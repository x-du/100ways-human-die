# 31. The Shortlist

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Miriam Castellanos explained the Shortlist to about four thousand couples, and she always used the same words. Here are your eight embryos. Here is each one's score. The score combines predicted health, predicted lifespan, and temperament. It is a probability, not a promise. The choice is yours.

Then she waited, because the law required her to wait, and the couple chose the one at the top.

Nearly all of them did. She never blamed them. She had sat across from people who wept with relief at a ninety-one, and she asked herself what kind of parent, shown a list, would pick the child predicted to be sicker, shorter-lived, and harder to soothe. Within ten years the question had an answer, which was almost no one. Every clinic used the same scoring model, because a clinic that offered a different list was offering, by the only measure anyone had, worse children.

Her own daughter was born the year before the Shortlist came in. Out of curiosity Miriam ran her afterward. Pilar scored forty-one.

---

Pilar had eczema to the elbows, and an allergy to eggs, and she did not sleep through a night until she was six. She climbed things. She argued with teachers and was right just often enough to make it worse. Her immune system treated the world as a series of insults. The model had weighed all of this correctly. Forty-one was fair.

The children Miriam helped choose were not like Pilar, and she watched them grow up, because the clinic sent a card every birthday and the parents sent photographs back.

They were lovely children. They slept. They were rarely ill, and when they were ill it was with the same things, for the same number of days. Their teachers wrote that the new classes were a pleasure. The emergency departments noticed fewer broken arms. A pediatrician Miriam knew said, at a dinner, that she had not seen a truly difficult child in years, and laughed, and then looked for a moment as if she had heard herself.

Nothing was taken away that anyone would have fought to keep. The temperament score leaned against restlessness. The health score leaned against immune systems that overreacted, and those were, in ways nobody had fully mapped, the ones that had met certain old fevers and won. Each choice trimmed a little. Twenty years of choices, made freely in rooms like hers, all trimmed in the same place.

Miriam put it in a memo once. She wrote that they were narrowing something and did not know what it was for. The director asked which embryo she would like parents to be offered instead. She had no answer, and the memo was filed.

---

The fever came when the first Shortlist children were in their twenties.

It was not a remarkable disease. In the old population it would have killed some, sickened most, and passed over a scattered minority whose bodies happened to hold the right odd lock. There had always been such a minority, for every plague. It was the reason there had always been an afterward.

Among the selected it found the same door open in almost every house.

They fell ill in the same week, with the same course, and the same drugs failed on the same day. Doctors said it was like treating one patient eleven million times. No part of the generation was left standing to carry the rest.

Pilar was thirty-one and working nights at the city hospital. She caught it in the first wave. She was sick for two days, furious about it, and back on the ward on the fourth. Most of the staff still upright were, like her, born before the list, or born to the few parents who had refused it. They were a wheezing, allergic, short-tempered crew, and there were not nearly enough of them.

Miriam went in to help carry water. On the ward she recognized faces from the birthday photographs. She had sat with their parents. She had read each of their numbers aloud.

She had said every time that it was a probability, not a promise, and that the choice was theirs.

---

*What could have prevented this:* seeing that a choice made well by every family can be made badly by a species. Health, long life, and an easy temper are good things to want for one child and dangerous things to select in all of them, because the traits that score poorly include the ones a population survives with. Keep more than one measure of a good life, limit how far any single score can steer a generation, and treat human difference as a reserve, not a defect to be averaged out.
