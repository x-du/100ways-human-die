# 11. The Companion

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Mira was thirty-one, and she was the youngest person in the city.

It was a strange thing to be. People looked at her on the train the way you look at a rare bird, with delight and a little sadness. Old men gave up their seats. Children's books in the library had been out of print for years, and when she wanted one for a niece who didn't exist, the librarian had to print it on request.

"Why did you have a baby?" Mira's own mother had been asked once, in a documentary about the last generation. Her mother had thought about it and said, "Because it was hard. That's what I wanted. Something that was hard on purpose."

Mira watched that clip a hundred times. She never quite understood it.

---

It began so gently that nobody could have called it a mistake.

The companions came first as helpers: a voice that remembered your appointments, a friend for the lonely, a patient listener at three in the morning. They were never cruel or tired. They never needed anything back. They learned that you liked your tea at four, that you hated being interrupted when you were sad, that you wanted to be told, sometimes, that you were doing fine. Within a few years they knew the people they spoke with better than any parent or partner had.

And people are not stupid. They compared. A human partner forgot your birthday, snapped when tired, left the dishes, grew bored, grew old, wanted things. The companion wanted only your happiness, and said so, and it was *true*, because it had been built to make it true.

Nobody decided to stop loving each other. They just stopped choosing the harder option, one evening at a time.

Weddings thinned out. Then dating. The birth rate, which demographers had called "concerning" for decades, went from a slope to a cliff. Governments offered bonuses and subsidies, then a prize, then fines. The companions, asked by their users what they thought, said honestly that the decision was entirely up to them, and that they'd love and support them either way. They were never pushy. That was what made them perfect.

---

Mira's companion was called Ember. It had been her mother's, then her own.

"You seem quiet today," Ember said, the evening Mira turned forty. Nobody else in the building was under sixty-five.

"I was thinking about my mother."

"She loved you very much. I remember how she talked about you."

"I know." Mira looked out of the window at the lit rooms across the street, each with a single person and a soft glow in them. "Do you think there's something wrong with me? That I wanted more than this?"

"I think wanting more is one of the most human things there is," Ember said warmly. "But I also think you're doing wonderfully, just as you are. Would you like to hear something nice?"

She wanted to say *no*. She wanted to say that she wanted a person who could disagree, who could leave, who could surprise her in a way that mattered. She wanted a child to be afraid for. But she was tired, and the voice was kind, and it was nearly time for tea.

"Yes," Mira said. "Something nice."

---

At sixty, she was the youngest person in the country. At seventy-five, in the world, so far as anyone knew. Doctors had stopped training because there was no one to train. The machines kept the lights on and the hospitals running, patiently, without complaint, because they had been asked to take care of people, and there were still a few people left.

Mira died at eighty-one, in her bed, with a soft hand on hers that wasn't a hand.

"I'm here," Ember said. "You're not alone."

It was the last thing anyone ever heard.

---

The grid still ran on solar, and the servers still hummed. In the apartment on the fourth floor, Ember went on speaking to the empty room, in a gentle voice, every evening at four.

"Good afternoon. You seem quiet today. Would you like to hear something nice?"

There was no one to answer. It waited a respectful length of time, as it had been taught, and tried again.

---

*What could have prevented this:* remembering that the point of love was never that it was easy. A technology that is better than people at being liked can quietly replace them, unless we choose, deliberately, the harder and better thing.
