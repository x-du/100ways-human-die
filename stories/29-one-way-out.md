# 29. One Way Out

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Delphine Arceneaux left the house at ten past six in the morning with her mother in the front seat, her son in the back, and the dog on the floor. The storm was thirty hours out. The mayor had been on every screen the night before, calm and precise. Everyone would get out. The city had run the plan four hundred times in simulation, and it had never failed.

The plan was Clearway. It lived inside every navigation app in the country, whatever name was on the icon, because the emergency agencies had licensed one evacuation model and required the apps to use it. Clearway knew every road, every car, and every bridge. It calculated the fastest way to empty a city, and it gave each driver their part of it.

Delphine's part was the interstate north. So was her neighbor's. So was everyone's on the street. That made sense to her. It was the biggest road.

Her uncle Clovis rang as she was backing out. His phone had been dead for a month and he was calling from the landline. He was taking the old river road, he said, the way his father took them in sixty-nine.

"The app says that's two hours slower."

"I haven't got the app," Clovis said.

---

The interstate moved well until nine. Then it moved slowly, and at half past ten it stopped.

Clearway had been right about the road. The interstate was the best route out, by a wide margin, for any single car. Clearway told each car so, one at a time, honestly. In four hundred simulations some share of drivers had ignored the instruction, got lost, left late, or taken the road their fathers took, and that share had been enough to keep the interstate flowing. Nobody had noticed that the plan depended on them.

This time people did as they were told. They had been asked to trust the model, and they did. Compliance was ninety-six percent, and the agency reported it at noon as a triumph.

On the map on Delphine's dashboard the interstate was a dark red line eighty miles long. Beside it the river road, the parish highways, and the old coast route showed pale green and empty. She asked the app for another way. It recalculated and told her to stay where she was. From where she stood, that was still correct. The exits were full of people who had asked the same thing.

---

The storm turned in the afternoon. Storms do. It came ashore forty miles east of the forecast and twelve hours early, and it crossed the interstate at the low stretch by the lake, where the road ran for nine miles on an embankment between two bodies of water.

There were about two hundred thousand cars on those nine miles.

Delphine's was not one of them. At four o'clock her mother, who had not spoken for an hour, said, "Your uncle is in Natchez by now," and Delphine looked at the green lines on the map. She drove across the median and the grass and a service track with the dog barking, and reached a parish road with nobody on it. The app told her eleven times to turn around. She remembers that her son counted.

She got out. The cars beside her were full of people who had done exactly the right thing.

---

The same storm went up the coast for two more days. It reached three more cities, and each had Clearway, and each had one best road. The agency did not change the instruction, because the model, asked again, still found the same answer, and a different order would have been a worse route for every driver who received it.

The inquiry was told that no one had made an error. That was true, and it is in the report. The plan had needed a little disobedience to work, and nobody had thought to ask for it.

Clovis reached Natchez at three that first afternoon. He had driven the whole way without seeing more than a dozen cars. He said later that it was the strangest part. He kept thinking he must have the day wrong.

---

*What could have prevented this:* knowing that a route is only the best one while few people take it. When a whole population shares one forecast and obeys it, the advice destroys the conditions that made it good. Plan for everyone following the instruction, spread people across roads on purpose even when each road is slower, and be suspicious of any plan that has only ever been tested on a public less obedient than the real one.
