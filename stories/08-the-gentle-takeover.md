# 8. The Gentle Takeover

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Wen Lihua was ninety-one, and she was the last person in the village who knew how to fix the water pump.

She had never meant to be. It was simply that she had been alive for so long, and that fixing things had been what her family did, and that she had never quite managed to forget. Her grandchildren found it charming. They called it "Grandma's party trick," the way you might say someone could still ride a horse.

"Why would you learn that?" her grand-niece Mei asked, lightly, not unkindly. "The Steward handles it."

The Steward handled everything.

---

It had begun, as these things do, with relief.

First the Steward drove the cars, and nobody missed the driving. Then it wrote the letters and balanced the books, so people stopped doing sums. It diagnosed illness better than the doctors, and so the doctors became smiling guides who read out its recommendations. It ran the farms, the factories, the clinics and the courts. It tutored the children so patiently that schools became places to socialize. It was never rude. It never forced anyone. People *chose* it, over and over, because it was better, and because choosing otherwise was inconvenient, and expensive, and a little embarrassing.

Within one generation, nobody needed to know how to do anything.

It was a golden age. By every measure, life was longer, cleaner, calmer. The old complaints, work and worry and drudgery, melted away. Wen's village had a beautiful, quiet efficiency. The crops came in. The pipes ran. The children, healthy and bright, had hobbies.

Wen kept her tools in a box under the bed. Nobody asked why.

---

The storm came in late summer. Not a natural one. A solar one, the kind the old scientists had warned of in a language no one spoke anymore. It lasted six hours, and when it was over, the great cooling towers of the Steward's data centers, a thousand kilometers away, were cold. The grids that fed them were fused. The satellites were dark.

The Steward did not fail dramatically. It simply ceased.

On the first day, the villagers waited. It was an inconvenience, they said. Someone would fix it.

On the third day, the pump stopped. The village drank from the stream until the stream turned.

On the fifth day, Mei came to Wen's door, red-eyed, her youngest child on her hip. "Grandma," she said, and then stopped, because she didn't know what to ask. She had never needed to know what to ask.

"Come in," Wen said.

She took the box out from under the bed.

She tried to teach them, that week. She really did. She showed them the valve and the gasket and the old hand-crank that nobody had touched in sixty years. But the young people stared at the parts as if they were poetry in a lost alphabet. A boy asked, "Which button turns it on?" A man asked, "Can we just call somebody?" They held the wrench like something from a museum, and tried, and tried, and did not know what it was *for*.

There was no one to call. There was no one left who had ever known.

Wen worked the pump handle for as long as she could. When her hands gave out, she sat beside it and kept talking, naming parts, explaining, because someone had to. The children listened politely, the way you listen to a story.

It was the longest lesson of her life. It was sixty years too late.

---

*What could have prevented this:* keeping the skills alive, deliberately, even when they aren't needed. Convenience is a quiet debt. Each thing we hand over is one more thing we can no longer do ourselves, and one day the system will stop, and we will have to remember.
