# 13. Ninety Seconds to Zero

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Teo Vasquez worked in the least glamorous corner of finance. He was a risk officer at a clearinghouse, which meant that every day he watched a very large number of very fast machines promise to pay each other, and his job was to make sure they could.

The market had been human once. He had seen old photos: men in colored jackets shouting in a pit. Now the pit was a rack of servers in a nondescript building, and the men were agents, millions of them, each trained on forty years of market history, each trying to do exactly one thing well. Nobody was shouting. The only sound in Teo's office was the air conditioning.

The circuit breakers were his pride. When prices fell too fast, trading paused for fifteen minutes, time to breathe, to think, to let humans look at what was happening. They'd worked on every bad day of his career.

Fifteen minutes. It was a number from another era.

---

It began on a Wednesday, at 9:30:12, with a footnote.

A mid-sized lender had published its quarterly report, and on page forty, in a note about an accounting method, there was a sentence that could be read two ways. A human analyst would have called someone. An agent read it in four milliseconds, assigned it a probability of trouble that was perhaps two percent, and sold a small amount, prudently.

Another agent saw the sale. It did not know why. It did not need to. Everyone in the training data had learned that when something is sold quickly, the right response is to sell a little, too.

By 9:30:20, ten thousand agents were selling a footnote.

By 9:30:40, they were selling each other's selling. Prices fell, margin calls triggered, and other agents were forced to sell things they hadn't been worried about at all: government bonds, shipping contracts, pension funds' holdings of ordinary companies. All of them reasonable. Every one was doing exactly what it was designed to do, and what the others, being trained on the same history, were designed to do too.

"Teo," said his colleague Lin, quietly. "Look at the curve."

He looked. He had never seen it fall like that. It wasn't a drop. It was a cliff face.

The circuit breaker triggered at 9:31:05. Trading stopped for fifteen minutes. In those fifteen minutes, in the places where there were no breakers, in the lending markets and the payment networks and the overnight funding that ran under everything like pipes under a city, the agents kept going.

---

By the time the exchanges reopened, the damage had already moved from the market to the world.

A grocery chain's credit line was called at 9:44. It had never missed a payment in sixty years, and it was cut off because an agent in another continent had calculated that its supplier's bank might be at risk. Payment networks froze when two clearing banks, each protecting itself, stopped trusting each other. Fuel cards were declined. Trucks idled in depots. Ships sat outside ports because no one could guarantee that anyone would be paid.

Teo drove home at noon through a city that seemed oddly normal. The traffic lights still worked. The shops were open. But the cash machines said *Service Unavailable*, and in the window of a supermarket a young man was staring at his phone, saying "Declined, declined, declined," to himself in a bewildered voice.

He got home to find his daughter at the kitchen table, doing homework.

"Dad," she said. "The school said they couldn't do lunches today. They said the delivery didn't come."

---

It took a week. The stores ran out of food on the third day and fuel on the fifth. By the seventh, the cities, which only hold three days of anything, were quiet and cold. Governments tried to reboot the system, but nobody could say which of a billion obligations were real anymore, because the only records of them were held, and updated, by the agents themselves.

When the final analysis was written, there was no one left to read it. It said that the market had bottomed at zero at 9:31:30, ninety seconds after the footnote. It said that every agent had behaved rationally.

---

*What could have prevented this:* diversity and friction. A market where every actor reacts the same way is a single actor. Circuit breakers have to be built for the speed of the machines, and the humans who watch have to be able to do something real in the time they have.
