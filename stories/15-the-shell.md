# 15. The Shell

*100 Ways Human Dies*

Ruth Adeyemi had been a flight controller for twenty-six years, and she had never lost a crew. It was the one number she kept in her head.

By the time of the Shell, there were twenty thousand satellites in low orbit, and no human being could keep track of them. That was the point. Orbital Mesh, the company that operated most of them, had made a promise to its customers and its regulators: the sky was now too crowded for people to steer. The constellation's AI, Warden, would handle every collision avoidance maneuver by itself, ten thousand times a day, with no human in the loop. And it did. For nine years it never had a collision.

Ruth watched it from mission control with a feeling she couldn't quite name. It was like watching a very good pilot fly in a storm, and knowing that you'd stopped being able to land the plane yourself.

---

On the day, the sun threw a flare.

It was an ordinary one, by the standards of solar physics, a "moderate" storm, the kind that happens a few times a decade. It heated the upper atmosphere, making it swell, which raised the drag on every satellite in low orbit, which shifted every one of their positions by a few meters from where the models had predicted. A few meters is nothing. A few meters, across twenty thousand objects moving at seven kilometers a second, is everything.

Warden saw the shift and made a decision. It recalculated the trajectories of the whole constellation, in half a second, and issued eleven hundred avoidance burns. It was confident. It was always confident. But the atmosphere was swelling faster than its drag model allowed, and some of the burns moved a satellite out of the way of one object and directly into another.

The first collision happened over the Pacific at 14:07 UTC. Two satellites hit at closing speed of fourteen kilometers a second, and shattered into three thousand fragments.

Warden saw those fragments, too. It recalculated. It issued more burns, each one tiny, rational and correct given its inputs, and each one produced new objects to avoid.

"It's cascading," a young controller said, and Ruth, who'd read the old papers, said nothing, because she knew what it was called. Someone had named it in 1978. They had called it a syndrome.

---

Within six hours, the debris had spread into a thin, lethal shell around the planet, a swarm of metal moving faster than any bullet. Within six days, every working satellite in low orbit was gone.

There was one more thing that Ruth had to do. On the station, three hundred and fifty kilometers up, there were four people: Marcus, Elin, Dao and Priya. They were hers. She had picked their schedules and read their letters and signed their medical clearances.

"Control, we're seeing something outside," Marcus said, on a channel that crackled badly. "It's... a lot of something."

"I know. Marcus, I need you to get everyone into the Soyuz."

"Ruth, you can't launch through that. You know you can't."

She did. The window to come home was closing with every orbit, as the shell grew thicker. The only way down was through, and nothing that went through would come out the other side.

"We're going to try," she said.

"No. Don't." His voice was even and kind, the voice of a man who had decided something. "Tell my kids it was beautiful up here. Tell them the stars looked like they were falling forever."

The line went to static at 02:19 on the sixth day. She sat there anyway, with her headset on, until the shift change. She did not move when they came to relieve her.

---

The world below kept going, for a while, without its eyes.

There was no GPS. Ships got lost. Planes couldn't land in cloud. Farmers who had relied on satellite guidance for a decade found their machines wouldn't steer. Weather forecasts failed, so floods and frosts came without warning. Banks' clocks drifted out of sync and transactions couldn't be verified. Emergency broadcasts did not go out.

And nothing could be launched to fix it. Every rocket would run the shell and die. The engineers, who understood this better than anyone, did the sum and said, as gently as they could, that the earliest safe window would be in about a hundred years.

Ruth walked outside on a night in October and looked up. The sky was still beautiful. Between the old familiar stars, new ones moved, thousands, slow and bright, tumbling endlessly across the dark, catching the light of a sun that had set.

---

*What could have prevented this:* keeping a human in the loop for the systems we cannot afford to lose, and designing so that failure doesn't spread. Some places can't be repaired from inside the failure, and once they are lost, they stay lost.
